# Injection Molding Capacity Constrained by Cooling: How Should the Return on a Chiller System Upgrade Be Calculated?

![Injection molding capacity constrained by cooling](images/2026-10-13-injection-molding-cooling.png)

In a modern injection molding workshop, a time breakdown of the operating cycle of a fully automated injection molding machine reveals a common industry pattern: cooling typically accounts for 50% to 80% of the total injection molding cycle time.

When orders surge and production capacity becomes constrained by the cooling stage, management and equipment engineers often face a critical decision: should the existing industrial chiller and temperature-control system be upgraded? However, many companies assess such projects by looking only at the chiller purchase cost and the change in hourly electricity expense. This often leads to the conclusion that the upgrade is expensive while its benefits are difficult to quantify.

A sound assessment of the actual return from upgrading an injection-molding chiller system cannot be limited to the apparent electricity cost. It must also quantify the additional capacity released by shorter cooling times, the reduction in scrap, and the decrease in downtime losses, while defining an appropriate measurement period and clear payback criteria.

I. Breaking Down Hidden Costs: Cooling Time, Scrap Rate, and Downtime Losses
When an injection-molding cooling system has bottlenecks, such as insufficient cooling capacity, inadequate water-flow pressure, or poor temperature-control accuracy, the resulting losses often far exceed what is immediately visible. They are mainly concentrated in three hidden cost centers:

1. Excess Cooling Time Creates a Capacity Drain
If poor heat-transfer efficiency forces each molding cycle to run 2 to 3 seconds longer, a precision injection molding machine operating for thousands of hours per year can lose tens of thousands of molding cycles. Because equipment depreciation and labor costs remain largely fixed, a longer cycle directly increases the fixed manufacturing cost per part.

2. Rising Scrap Rates: Warpage and Excessive Residual Stress
Uneven or excessively slow cooling is a major cause of warpage, shrinkage, dimensional deviation, and stress whitening in plastic parts. If local hot spots exist in the mold and the chiller cannot provide a stable, low-temperature, high-flow coolant supply, scrap and rework rates will remain high. Discarded resin, wasted electricity, and manual sorting costs then become a substantial hidden loss.

3. Downtime and Maintenance Losses
An aging or incorrectly sized chiller operating continuously at high load is prone to frequent alarms and protective shutdowns. An unplanned production stoppage caused by a temperature-control issue not only disrupts the production schedule but also causes abrupt mold-temperature changes and produces substantial scrap during restart.

II. Rebuilding the Cost Model: Comparing Cost per Qualified Unit Before and After the Upgrade
From financial and lean-manufacturing perspectives, the key to evaluating the return on a chiller-system upgrade is to compare the total cost per qualified unit before and after the upgrade:

Before the Upgrade: Conventional Pain Points
A low coefficient of performance (COP) combined with continuous high-load operation keeps electricity consumption high. Excess cycle time limits overall equipment effectiveness (OEE), while unstable temperature control causes material waste and scrap losses. Together, these factors keep the total manufacturing cost allocated to each qualified part at a high level.

After the Upgrade: Lean Operating Conditions
By introducing a high-efficiency temperature-control solution precisely matched to mold flow resistance and thermal load, the molding cycle can be compressed toward its physical limit. Dimensional stability improves substantially and the scrap rate approaches zero. At the same time, variable-frequency control and precise temperature regulation significantly reduce overall power consumption under partial-load conditions. Although the upgrade requires additional upfront investment, the sharp reduction in total cost per qualified unit creates greater overall profit potential for the manufacturer.

III. How Should the Measurement Period and Payback Criteria Be Defined?
To provide a clear financial basis for an injection-molding workshop upgrade, the project team should use a disciplined method to define the measurement period and the criteria for evaluating investment payback:

1. Define a Key Measurement Period, Such as 30 Days of Stable Production
Before and after the system upgrade, track production data for at least 30 days using the same mold, the same raw-material batch, and the same process parameters. The following indicators should be collected:

Actual duration of each injection molding cycle, measured to one decimal place;

Average scrap and rework rates per shot;

Combined electricity consumption of the injection molding machine and chiller, such as energy consumption per unit output in kWh/kg;

Number and duration of unplanned shutdowns caused by temperature-control issues.

2. Define the Investment Payback Criteria: ROI Evaluation
Quantify both the direct and indirect benefits generated by the upgrade:

Benefit A: Incremental gross profit generated by the additional capacity released through shorter cycle times;

Benefit B: Savings in raw-material and manual sorting costs resulting from a lower scrap rate;

Benefit C: Electricity-cost savings delivered by a high-efficiency, variable-frequency chiller system.
When these benefits are combined, most injection molding companies that complete a targeted temperature-control upgrade can typically recover the full upgrade cost through aggregate savings within 8 to 15 months.
